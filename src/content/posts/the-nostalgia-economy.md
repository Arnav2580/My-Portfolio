Watching _Napoleon_ on Apple TV sent me back to reading about the French Revolution. Something I once associated with school suddenly felt fascinating. The subject had not changed, but the context had: I was following my curiosity rather than preparing for an exam.

That experience made me think about nostalgia. A film, song, or object can bring us back to an earlier part of our lives. It can also make an unfamiliar past feel worth exploring. Those are different experiences, but both can turn history and culture into present-day attention.

An old song can matter because of who we heard it with. A childhood toy can represent a friendship, a home, or a time when life felt different. A vintage jacket might be a fashion choice, but it might also connect someone to their family or an imagined version of another era. The object and the attachment are not the same thing, yet both can influence its value.

That is how I understand the nostalgia economy: the commercial activity that forms around people's emotional relationships with the past. It is a useful lens for studying demand, rather than a neatly bounded industry. To understand it, we need two kinds of evidence: research into why nostalgia affects people, and market data showing where related purchases take place.

## What the research says about memory and belonging

Psychologists Kristin Layous and Jaime Kurtz describe nostalgia as a mixed emotion, usually more positive than negative, associated with personally meaningful memories and close relationships. Their 2023 review considers its potential contribution to well-being. That helps explain why a familiar object can carry significance beyond its function: it may be connected to people and experiences that matter to its owner. [Research: Nostalgia: A potential pathway to greater well-being](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2022.101548)

A separate 2023 review by Andrew Abeyta and Simran Pillarisetty examines evidence that nostalgia can help people find, maintain, and restore meaning in life. These findings concern psychological experience; they are not measurements of sales or evidence that every nostalgic advertisement succeeds. [Research: Nostalgia supports a meaningful life](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2022.101520)

The connection to spending has also been tested directly. In six experiments published in the _Journal of Consumer Research_, Jannine Lasaleta, Constantine Sedikides, and Kathleen Vohs examined nostalgia and the desire for money. One experiment found greater willingness to pay for products among participants in the nostalgia condition; other experiments used different measures of how participants valued money. The authors linked the effect to feelings of social connectedness. [Research: Nostalgia Weakens the Desire for Money](https://doi.org/10.1086/677227)

That provides evidence for a possible consumer mechanism, not a universal pricing rule. An experimentally induced feeling is different from an actual purchase made within a household budget. It does not tell a founder that adding a retro design will reliably increase revenue, or how large that increase would be.

A systematic review in the _Journal of Advertising_ examined 205 articles and proposed factors that could influence nostalgic appeals, including purchase context and culture. That breadth is a reminder to ask which audience, which memory, and which situation an appeal is meant to address. [Research: Nostalgia: A Review, Propositions, and Future Research Agenda](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2022.2101036)

## From a feeling to a purchase

My interpretation is that nostalgia can create several different kinds of value. It can reconnect a buyer with a personal memory, give a group something to share, or make an object feel consistent with the person's identity. A record collection, for example, can be both private enjoyment and a way of expressing taste to others.

There are competing explanations, too. A young customer buying an old camera may like its appearance without remembering the era it came from. A collector may be motivated by rarity or resale potential. A fan buying merchandise for a new release may have no nostalgic attachment at all. These motives can coexist, and a sales total cannot separate them.

This is why I would avoid calculating a single nostalgia-market total by adding toys, music, collectibles, and resale. Their boundaries overlap, their measurement methods differ, and much of their demand has other causes. The numbers below are evidence about relevant markets, not a measure of spending attributable to nostalgia.

## Music: physical formats are growing alongside streaming

IFPI's Global Music Report 2026 puts worldwide recorded-music trade revenue at **$31.7 billion in 2025**, up **6.4%**. Paid subscription streaming accounted for **52.4%** of revenue and remained a major growth driver. This describes the recording industry, including new releases and contemporary listening, rather than a nostalgia market. [Source: IFPI, March 2026](https://www.ifpi.org/global-music-report-2026-global-recorded-music-revenues-grow-6-4-as-record-companies-drive-innovation/)

For **January-June 2026**, the RIAA reports US vinyl revenue of **$543.8 million**, up **17.7%** against the same period in 2025. Vinyl units increased **20.9%**, to **26.5 million**. CD revenue reached **$171.1 million**, up **58.6%**. Streaming still generated roughly **$4.9 billion**, so physical-format growth does not establish that listeners are abandoning streaming. These are US wholesale figures, net of returns. [Source: RIAA, 2026 mid-year report](https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/2026-Mid-Year-Music-Industry-Revenue-Report.pdf)

The measurement basis matters: the RIAA's 2025 year-end report explains its move to **wholesale reporting**. Historical retail-value totals should not be compared directly with these newer wholesale figures. [Source: RIAA, 2025 year-end report](https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/RIAA-Year-End-Revenue-2025.pdf)

My interpretation is that physical ownership can offer something streaming does not: an object to display, give, collect, or associate with an experience. Nostalgia is one possible motivation. Attachment to a current artist, artwork, or new release is another. Format sales alone cannot tell us why someone bought a record.

## Toys: adult demand is real, but definitions matter

Circana's June 2026 Global Toy Report release estimates worldwide toy sales at **$123 billion in 2025**, up **8%**. That is the whole toy industry, not the size of adult collecting or nostalgia. [Source: Circana, June 2026](https://www.circana.com/post/global-toy-market-hits-123b-as-play-expands-across-ages-and-markets)

For the **first half of 2026**, Circana reports US toy dollar sales up **17%**, with units up **12%**. Sales for recipients aged **18 and over** grew **25%**. Licensed toys grew **24%** and represented **39%** of toy sales. These figures support the importance of adult participation and entertainment properties, without identifying how much spending was motivated by childhood memories. [Source: Circana, August 2026](https://www.circana.com/post/us-toy-industry-posts-strongest-first-half-in-six-years-circana-reports)

A study of recipients aged 12 and over includes teenagers; it is not an adults-only measure. Purchases by households without children are also different from purchases intended for adults, because those households can buy gifts for children. A neat "kidult market" headline can hide these distinctions.

Circana's 2025 review also reports collectible-toy sales up **32%**, accounting for almost **19%** of toy dollar sales in the context of its tracked markets. Its G12 coverage comprises twelve countries, not every market worldwide. This is evidence about collectible toys, not a valuation of everything people collect. [Source: Circana, January 2026](https://www.circana.com/post/global-toy-industry-rebounds-in-2025-as-sales-rise-7-fueled-by-pop-culture-collectibles-and-kidu)

## Resale: an older object does not imply a nostalgic buyer

ThredUp's 2026 Resale Report, conducted by GlobalData, says the US secondhand clothing market grew nearly **four times as fast** as the broader retail clothing market in 2025. It projects the US market to reach **$78.8 billion by 2030**. That is a forecast, not a measured current market size. The report combines market modeling with research including a survey of **3,268 US adults**, and is published by a company participating in resale. [Source: ThredUp / GlobalData, April 2026](https://newsroom.thredup.com/news/thredup-14th-resale-report)

Vintage style can carry emotional meaning, but secondhand shopping has other plausible motivations: a discontinued design, a lower price, better materials within a budget, or an item unavailable elsewhere. Two people can buy the same jacket for entirely different reasons.

That changes the business opportunity. A seller who assumes every buyer wants a story may neglect what the customer actually needs: reliable sizing, condition information, authenticity, fair pricing, and delivery.

## What this means to me as a builder

My interpretation is that nostalgia can help a product earn attention, while its quality and experience determine whether that attention becomes a lasting relationship. The reports show activity in relevant categories; they do not prove this mechanism on their own.

If I were testing a business around nostalgia, I would start with a specific community and attachment. Which era, character, object, or ritual matters to them? Are they looking for a memory, a social connection, a design they miss, or the pleasure of owning something tangible?

Then I would test willingness to pay and repeat behavior. Do customers return after the initial excitement? Does a familiar reference improve conversion compared with an otherwise similar product? What do interviews reveal about the purchase? Can the business make money after sourcing, licensing, authentication, shipping, and returns?

These questions matter more than attaching a huge market total to an emotion. They also leave room for different generations to value the same thing differently. One customer may remember a character from childhood; another may be discovering it for the first time.

There are limits. Familiarity can become repetitive. Scarcity can attract speculation without building lasting demand. A revived product can disappoint if the experience does not match the memory. I would treat nostalgia as a hypothesis about customer motivation that needs testing, rather than a substitute for product quality.

## A lasting role for the past

I still think the business of memory is worth studying. Current reports show physical-music growth, adult participation in toys, and expansion in secondhand clothing. They provide places to investigate nostalgia's influence, not a combined measurement of it.

For me, nostalgia is neither automatically a marketing trick nor a standalone industry with a reliable price tag. It is one way people assign meaning to things. A business can exploit that meaning briefly, or create something that deserves the attachment.

> The opportunity is to understand what people want to carry forward from the past.

My evening with _Napoleon_ began with renewed curiosity. It ended with a better question: when people return to something familiar, what are they really looking for, and can we create something worth returning to?
